                                                SUBFACTORS AND RICHARD THOMPSON’S GROUPS

                                                                                ARNAUD BROTHIER

                                               Abstract. Vaughan Jones discovered unexpected connections between Richard Thomp-
                                               son’s group and subfactor theory while attempting to construct conformal field theories
                                               (in short CFT). Among other this founded Jones’ technology: a powerful new method
                                               for constructing actions of fraction groups which had numerous applications in mathe-
arXiv:2207.03100v2 [math.GR] 17 Oct 2022




                                               matical physics, operator algebras, group theory and more surprisingly in knot theory
                                               and noncommutative probability theory.
                                                  We propose and outline a program in the vein of Jones’ work but where the Thompson
                                               group is replaced by a family of groups that we name forest-skein groups. These groups
                                               are constructed from diagrammatic categories, are tailor-made for using Jones’ technol-
                                               ogy, capture key aspects of the Thompson group, and aim to better connect subfactors
                                               with CFT. Our program strengthens Jones’ visionary work and moreover produces a
                                               plethora of concrete groups which satisfy exceptional properties.
                                                  In this first article we introduce the general theory of forest-skein groups, provide
                                               criteria of existence, give explicit presentations, prove that their first `2 -Betti number
                                               vanishes, construct a canonical action on a totally ordered set, establish a topological
                                               finiteness theorem showing that many of our groups are of type F∞ , and finish by study-
                                               ing a beautiful class of explicit examples.

                                      Introduction
We start by explaining how subfactor theory, conformal field theory, and the groups of
Richard Thompson got connected. Second, we outline a general program: what do we
wish and what are our motivations and aims. Third, we explain our general formalism
based on the manipulation of diagrams and explain how it relates to existing constructions
in the literature of group theory. We end by describing the content of this present article
and briefly mentioning some works in progress.
Subfactor, planar algebra, conformal field theory, and braid. In the 1980’s
Vaughan Jones initiated subfactor theory which rapidly became a major field in oper-
ator algebras [Jon83]. Connections with mathematical physics naturally appeared (see
[EK92, Kaw18, Eva22]) but also with seemingly completely unrelated subjects such as
knot theory with the introduction of the Jones polynomial [Jon85, Jon87]. Jones worked
very hard in finding new point of views and powerful formalisms to study objects. This
led to the introduction of Jones’ planar algebras: a description of the standard invariant
of subfactors using diagrams similar to string diagrams in tensor categories [Jon99].
Richard Thompson’s groups F, T, and V . On a rather different part of mathematics
lives the three Richard Thompson group F ⊂ T ⊂ V [CFP96]. These groups naturally
appear in various parts of mathematics such as logic, topology, dynamics, complexity
theory to name a few. They follow rather unusual properties and display new phenomena
                                 FOREST-SKEIN GROUPS I                                    3

in group theory. For instance, the groups T and V were the first examples found of
finitely presented simple infinite groups. Moreover, Brown and Geoghegan proved that F
satisfied the topological finiteness property F∞ and was of infinite geometric dimension,
providing the first torsion-free group of this kind, see Section 6.4 for definitions [BG84].
These groups seem completely unrelated to subfactors although they share one common
feature: elements of Thompson’s groups can be described by planar diagrams (a pair of
rooted trees) as shown by Brown [Bro87].
From subfactors to almost CFT, and Thompson’s group T . In the 2010’s Jones
discovered an unexpected connection between subfactors and the Thompson groups [Jon17],
see also the survey [Bro20]. The story goes as follows. Subfactors and conformal field the-
ory (in short CFT) in the formalism of Doplicher-Haag-Roberts (DHR), have been closely
linked since the late 1980’s when Longo quickly realised that the Jones index is equal
to the square of the statistical dimension in DHR theory [DHR71, Lon89]. Moreover,
in the mid 1990’s Longo and Rehren prove that any CFT produces a subfactor [LR95].
However, the converse remains mysterious and is one of the most important question in
the field, see [EG11, Bis17, Xu18]. Jones famous question was: ”Does every subfactor has
something to do with conformal field theory?” There are some case by case reconstruction
results but the most exotic and interesting subfactors are not known to provide CFT at
the moment. Using planar algebras Jones constructed a field theory associated to each
subfactor that is not quite conformal but has a rather discrete group of symmetry. This
symmetry group is nothing else than Thompson’s group T .
New connections and Jones’ technology. From there, new field theories were in-
troduced but also connections between subfactors, Thompson’s groups, and braid groups
[Jon17, Jon18b]. For the last connection we invite the reader to consult the survey of
Jones [Jon19]. Moreover, Jones found an efficient technology for constructing actions of
Thompson’s group F using diagrams. Originally, this was done by transforming tree-
diagrams of Thompson group into string diagrams in a planar algebra or a nice tensor
category. Jones quickly realised that it could be greatly generalised founding Jones’ tech-
nology. It works as follows. Consider a monoid or a category C admitting a calculus of
fractions. This allows to formally inverting elements (or more precisely morphisms) of C
obtaining a fraction groupoid Frac(C) and fraction groups Frac(C, e) (that are isotropy
groups at object e). Now, the new part of Jones is that any functor Φ : C → D starting
from C and ending in any category can produce an action of Frac(C) and thus of Frac(C, e).
Hence, if a complicated group G can be expressed as Frac(C, e) from a somewhat simpler
category C, then we can produce many actions of G using the simpler structure C. For
instance, any isometry H → H ⊗ H between Hilbert spaces provides a unitary represen-
tation of F (which in fact extends to V ). This is done by applying Jones’ technology to
the categorical description of the Thompson groups. Moreover, Jones’ technology is very
explicit giving practical algorithms for extracting information of the action constructed
such as matrix coefficients in the case of unitary representations. Applications has been
given in various fields such as: knot theory, mathematical physics, general group the-
ory, study of Thompson’s groups, operator algebras, noncommutative probability theory
[Jon19, ABC21, Jon18a, BS20, BS19, Bro19a, BJ19b, BJ19a, KKW20, KK22].


Program: wishes, motivations, and aims. Our program is to consider groups G =
Frac(C, e) constructed from categories C of diagrams that are well-suited for approximating
4                                    ARNAUD BROTHIER

CFT, but also for applying Jones’ technology, and are interesting groups on their own.
More specifically:


     • (Calculus of fractions) We wish to be able to decide easily if a category of diagrams
       C admits a cancellative calculus of fractions requiring C to be left-cancellative and
       satisfying Ore’s property (i.e. if t, s ∈ C have same target, then there exists f, g ∈ C
       satisfying tf = sg). This permits to construct groups.
     • (From subfactors to CFT) Given any subfactor, Jones constructed from its planar
       algebra P a field theory where Thompson’s group T took the role of the space-time
       diffeomorphism group. With P fixed we wish to perform a similar construction
       but having a more sophisticate and specific symmetry group Frac(C, e).
     • (Exceptional group properties) Forgetting about CFT and subfactors we wish to
       construct fraction groups Frac(C, e) that are interesting on their own: groups
       satisfying some of the exceptional properties of the Thompson groups. This would
       provide new examples of rare phenomena in group theory but also would help
       understanding why the Thompson groups are so special.
     • (Applying Jones’ technology) We wish to apply efficiently Jones’ technology to the
       fraction groups Frac(C, e) for constructing actions. This would occur if C admits
       a concrete presentation with few generators as a category or better describe by
       explicit skein relations of diagrams (see below).
     • (Operator algebras) The Pythagorean algebra P was a C*-algebra that naturally
       appeared when applying Jones’ technology to the Thompson groups [BJ19a]. It
       provides a powerful method for constructing representations of the Cuntz alge-
       bra using only finite dimensional operators, new class of representations of the
       Thompson groups, and moreover exhibits a rare example of a non-nuclear C*-
       algebra satisfying the lifting property [Cun77, BJ19a, BW22b, BW22a, Cou21].
       We wish to extend these connections and applications in a larger setting.




Our class of diagrams and structures. Between forests and string diagrams.
Here is our choice that we believe fit into all the constraints and wishes enunciated above.
This class is inspired by the tree diagrams used by Brown to describe elements of Thomp-
son’s group F and string diagrams appearing in Jones’ planar algebras.
Recall that an element of F is described by a class of pairs of trees (t, s) that have
the same number of leaves. Here, tree means a finite ordered rooted binary tree. Two
such pairs defined the same element of F when they are equal up to removing or adding
corresponding carets on each tree. We often describe the element associated to (t, s) by
a diagram in the plane with the roots of t on the bottom and its leaves on top that are
connected to the leaves of s where s is placed upside down on top of t. Now removing a
pair of corresponding carets is the operation of removing a diamond as described in the

Elements of Jones’ planar algebras (or other similar structures like Etingof-Nikshych-
Ostrik’s fusion categories or rigid C*-tensor categories) are described by linear combi-
nations of string diagrams [Jon99, ENO05]. String diagrams are planar diagrams made
of a large outer disc, some inner labelled discs, and some non-intersecting strings that
start and end at certain boundary points of discs and perhaps some closed loops. Here is
an example with three inner discs having 4,2,4 boundary points (when read from top to
bottom and left to right) and one closed loop:

A class of such diagrams, as originally defined by Jones, is generated by a collection
of labelled n-valent vertices and satisfies relations expressed by linear combinations of
string diagrams constructed from the generators. We call the later skein relations (as in
the context of knot theory and Conway tangles) that are identities closed under taking
concatenation of diagrams. Explicit examples of skein relations of planar algebras can be
found for instance in [Pet10] or [MPS10, Section 3] or in the PhD thesis of Liu [Liu15].
Having such planar presentations for Jones’ planar algebras is powerful as it permits to
express in few symbols very rich algebraic structures.
We define classes of certain planar diagrams called forest-skein categories using forest
diagrams whose vertices are labelled and satisfy certain skein relations, although removing
any linear structures. The set of skein relations completely describes the forest-skein
category and provides a skein presentation.
Forest-skein categories. We consider all (finite ordered rooted binary) forests that we
represent as planar diagrams with roots on the bottom and leaves on top that we order
from left to right. We fix a set S and colour each interior vertex of a forest with an
element of S obtaining a coloured forest. Here is an example of coloured forest with three

Note, we do not colour the leaves just like boundary points of the exterior disc of a string
diagram is not coloured in Jones’ framework. This produces a small category (where
composition is given by horizontal stacking) that we call the free forest-skein category
over S. This category is always left-cancellative. Now, if S is a single colour, then we
recover the usual category of monochromatic forests whose fraction group is Thompson’s
group F . If S has more than two colours, then this category never satisfies Ore’s property
and thus does not admit any calculus of fractions nor produces fraction groups.
The idea for obtaining Ore’s property is to mod out by some relations similar to skein
relations. These relations are expressed by a pair of coloured trees (u, u0 ) that have the
same number of leaves. Now, two forests f, f 0 are equivalent if by substituting subtrees of
f isomorphic to u by u0 (or the opposite) we can transform f into f 0 . The quotient is a
small category F of equivalence classes of diagrams that we call a forest-skein category.
Such a F is expressed in a compact way by a skein presentation (S, R) where S is the
set of colours and R is a set of pairs of trees (u, u0 ) that are skein relations. A skein
presentation allows to apply Jones’ technology efficiently and to study in an effective
manner associated structures like fraction groups.

Forest-skein monoids. A skein presentation (S, R) defines a monoid F∞ := ForMhS|Ri
that we call a forest-skein monoid. It is obtained by considering infinite forests with roots
and leaves indexed by N>0 that have finitely many nontrivial trees. It is indeed a monoid
with composition being vertical stacking of diagrams. Moreover, note that this monoid
                                   FOREST-SKEIN GROUPS I                                        7

can be obtained from the forest-skein category by performing a certain inductive limit. It
is an auxiliary structure that is useful for studying F.
Forest-skein groups. In some occasion F is a Ore category (i.e. it is left-cancellative
and satisfies Ore’s property) and thus we may formally invert forests obtaining a fraction
groupoid Frac(F). Now, by considering any r ≥ 1 we have an isotropy group Frac(F, r)
corresponding to all f ◦g −1 where f, g have the same number of leaves and have r roots. We
choose a favourite group G := Frac(F, 1) (produced with trees) which we call the fraction
group of F and call all these groups forest-skein groups. Note that Frac(F, r) ≃ G for
any r since we work with binary forests (implying that the fraction groupoid Frac(F)
is path-connected and thus all its isotropy groups are isomorphic). Similarly, the forest-
skein monoid F∞ provides a fraction group H := Frac(F∞ ) that can be obtained as an
inductive limit of the Frac(F, r) (letting r tending to infinity). All these groups embed
in each other but are in general not isomorphic in an obvious way (unlike the classical
Thompson group case). Jones’ technology is more effective for studying G rather than H
and we thus focus more on G.
Previous constructions in the literature. Examples of groups constructed from
diagrams of forests with more than one caret have been previously considered. The first
appeared in the work of Stein by allowing carets of various degrees, then in the work
of Brin and the description of partition of hypercubes with several binary carets (one
caret per dimension), and more recently in the work of Burillo, Nucinkis, and Reeves in
the description of the Cleary irrational-slope Thompson group using two different binary
carets [Ste92, Bri04, BNR21]. They all act by transformation of the unit interval or
higher dimensional cube that are piecewise affine. We will come back to these examples
but before let’s say that the last example cited fits exactly into the framework of this
article, the first fits in an extension of it, and the second not quite as we will explain later.
Generalisation and extension of the formalism. In this first article we present the
general theory of forest-skein categories and their forest-skein groups for coloured binary
forests as briefly explained above. This produces forest-skein groups similar to Thomp-
son’s group F . We list below some classical tree-diagrams that have been considered to
construct Thompson-like groups by various authors. Each of these extension of Thomp-
son’s group F can be applied and combined in our framework of forest-skein categories
such as:
     • add permutations of leaves (obtaining Thompson’s groups T and V see [Bro87]);
     • replace binary forests by n-ary forests for any n ≥ 2 (by Higman and Brown
       obtaining Higman-Thompon’s groups Vn,r and the Brown subgroups Fn,r , Tn,r
       [Hig74, Bro87]);
     • allow forests whose vertices have various degrees (by Stein [Ste92]);
     • label leaves with elements of an auxiliary group (independently by Tanushevski,
       Witzel-Zaremsky, the author [Tan16, WZ18, Bro22a, Bro21]);
     • add braids on top of leaves (independently by Brin and Dehornoy obtaining the
       braided Thompson group BV [Bri07, Bri06, Deh06]);
     • perform more sophisticate Brin-Zappa-Szép products between forests and an auxil-
       iary category (by Witzel-Zaremsky via general cloning systems of groups [WZ18]).
All these extensions are interesting and rich in applications. We can adapt these construc-
tions in the more general framework of coloured forests with no more technical problems
than in the classical case of the Thompson groups. In this first article we stick to the binary
8                                   ARNAUD BROTHIER

case for simplicity and clarity of the exposition. Although, we do explain how to incor-
porate permutations and braids using the approaches of Brown and Brin [Bro87, Bri07].
This produces X-forest-skein categories denoted F X (for X = F, T, V, BV ) and X-forest-
skein groups similar to the three Thompson groups F, T, V and the braided Thompson
group BV .
Forest-skein groups among Thompson-like groups. Here, we want to locate our
class of forest-skein groups among various Thompson-like groups existing in the literature.
We have listed above a number of ways to generalise the notion of forest-skein groups but
here we discuss about the smaller class of forest-skein groups obtained from forest-skein
categories of coloured binary forests without any decoration on leaves, so no permutations,
braids, nor auxiliary group, (so manifestly constructed like F ). We start by giving prop-
erties of forest-skein groups we have observed, then list some known groups that happen
to be forest-skein groups, and finally discuss about few families of Thompson-like groups
that are related or not to our class of groups.
Properties and observations on forest-skein groups. A forest-skein group contains a copy
of F . In particular, they inherent various properties of F such as not being elementary
amenable, having exponential growth, and having infinite geometric dimension. Although,
they do not share all properties of F . Indeed, most of the examples we have encounter have
torsion in their abelianisation and have sometimes torsion themselves. There are some
forest-skein group that contains a copy of the free group of rank two. Some forest-skein
groups are nontrivial extension of F and so are their derived group (preventing them to
be simple). There exist forest-skein groups that decompose as nontrivial direct products
and can have some finite conjugacy classes or even a nontrivial center. Every forest-skein
group admits a nice simplicial complex on which it acts. Although, this complex does
not have any obvious cubical structure and we don’t know any CAT(0) cubical complex
on which a generic forest-skein group acts. However, via this complex, we will prove
that many forest-skein groups are of type F∞ : for each k ≥ 1 they admit a classifying
space with finitely many cells of dimension smaller than k. As commented below, most
Thompson-like groups have natural actions on an interval or a similar structure. No such
actions seem to exists for forest-skein groups but we will construct a canonical totally
ordered set on which they act.

Brin’s higher-dimensional Thompson groups and Belk-Zaremsky’s extensions of it. Brin
has constructed higher-dimensional Thompson groups dV for each dimension d ≥ 1 [Bri04,
Bri05]. Informally, this is done by considering piecewise affine bijections between standard
dyadic partitions of hypercubes of dimension d. An element of dV is described by two such
partitions with same number of pieces and one correspondence saying which piece is sent
to which. A partition is described by a binary tree having carets coloured by {1, · · · , d}
(one colour per dimension) and a labelling of the leaves corresponding to a labelling of
the pieces of the partition. Refining a partition corresponds to grow the associated tree.
Unfortunately, they seem to not exists any meaningful way for labelling the leaves of
the grown tree that is compatible with the labelling of the elements of the partitions,
see the original articles of Brin but also an article of Burillo and Cleary for additional
details [BC10]. Hence, we may define a forest-skein group admitting similar diagrammatic
description but it will not describe transformation of hypercubes and moreover are not
isomorphic to Brin’s groups, see Section 3.6. For d = 2, such a forest-skein group admits
the following skein presentation:

which translates the two way to cut a square into four equal pieces. Note that Brin’s
construction extends to any nonempty set S (including infinite sets) giving the group

Auxiliary group and morphism. Given a group Γ and group morphism φ : Γ → Γ ⊕ Γ one
can construct a Thompson-like group G(Γ, φ) obtained by taking binary (monochromatic)
trees, decorating their leaves with elements of Γ, and using φ for growing trees. This
was independently discovered by Tanushevski, Witzel-Zaremsky, and the author [Tan16,
WZ18, Bro22a]. See the appendix of [Bro22a] for details and a precise comparison of the
three approaches. We have discovered with surprise that this class of groups intersects
10                                   ARNAUD BROTHIER

nontrivially our class of forest-skein groups. The intersection we found between the two
classes is exactly the following. All groups G(Γ, φ) where φ(g) = (g, e) (e being the
neutral element of Γ) and Γ = ker(H → Z) where H is the fraction group of a Ore
monoid admitting a homogeneous presentation and where H → Z is the word-length map
of the presentation. We will present in details this connection in the immediate successor
of this article [Bro22b].
Guba-Sapir’s diagram groups. It is natural to wonder if forest-skein groups are related
to Guba-Sapir diagram groups [GS97]. Indeed, both classes are constructed by planar
diagrams similar to coloured forests (see in particular their dual diagrammatic repre-
sentations appearing in [GS97, Section 4]), are related to the Thompson groups, and
admit obvious T and V -versions. However, their resemblance seems misleading. Indeed,
all diagram groups (the F -version) are torsion-free with torsion-free abelianisations and
act freely properly and isometrically on a CAT(0) cubical complex in contrast with our
examples of forest-skein groups.
Hughes’ FSS groups. Note that picture groups (i.e. braided diagram groups in Guba-Sapir
terminology corresponding to Thompson-like group similar to V ) forms the same class
than Hughes’ finite similarity structure groups (in short FSS groups) by [Hug09, FH17].
Hence, from our observations of above, it seems that FSS groups and forest-skein groups
don’t have a large intersection.
Piecewise affine maps or action on trees. Many Thompson-like groups act as piece-
wise affine maps on an interval or a similar structure. This is the case for the Higman-
Thompson groups, Stein groups, Brin’s dV , and more generally a class of groups defined
by Farley and Hughes [FH20]. Similarly, they typically act on totally disconnected spaces
like the Cantor space of binary sequences (by swapping finite prefixes) or more gener-
ally as almost automorphisms of trees. The family of groups just mentioned act in that
way and other like Rover-Nekrashevych groups, Brin’s dV groups and its generalisations
like the one of Martinez-Perez and Nucinkis or Belk-Zaremsky’s twisted Brin-Thompson
groups [Rov99, Nek04, MN13, BZ22].
So far we have not find any canonical piecewise affine actions nor almost automorphism
actions on trees for a generic forest-skein group. By construction our class of groups differ
greatly from a number of previous families of Thompson-like groups and seems to behave
differently as well. Although, we found for any forest-skein group a canonical action on a
totally ordered space which playes the role of the dyadic rationals of the torus for F, T, V ,
see Section 6.
Thumann’s operad groups. In his PhD thesis Thumann defines operad groups which play
a special role in this article [Thu17]. A nice operad defines three operad groups: a planar,
symmetric, and braided one corresponding to F, V , and BV . The class of operad groups
is huge and as far as the author observed all Thompson-like groups similar to F, V, BV
can be naturally identified with operad groups including our class of forest-skein groups.
Examples that seem to not admit an obvious description as operad groups are the one
constructed using Brin-Zappa-Szép products as in the theory of cloning systems of Witzel-
Zaremsky such as Thompson group T constructed using cyclic permutations or V mock
constructed from mock-symmetric permutations that seat in between F and V , see [WZ18,
Section 9]. Although, all these examples are probably isomorphic to operad groups anyway
by encoding the Brin-Zappa-Szép product in the operad (like every group is the fraction
group of itself). Thumann provides a powerful abstract and very general framework.
Indeed, using some of Thumann’s result we deduce that many forest-skein groups are

of type F∞ (for any n ≥ 1 the group admits a classifying space with finite n-skeleton,

Content of this present article. In this present article we introduce the formalism
of forest-skein categories and their associated fraction groups called forest-skein groups.
In particular, we define skein presentations which will play a key role in applying Jones’
technology in future articles. We provide general criteria to decide that a forest-skein
category admits a calculus of fractions. This is a key strength of our formalism:
we are able to decide for many skein presentations if they can produce groups
or not. We present two canonical actions of forest-skein groups: one on a simplicial
complex giving a classifying space and another on a totally ordered space which mimics
the classical action of the Thompson groups on the set of dyadic rationals of the unit
torus or interval. We deduce explicit presentations of forest-skein groups.
We do not apply Jones’ technology nor explore connections with CFT in this article.
Instead, we consider an exceptional property that the Thompson groups F, T, V share.
Indeed, we prove that a large class of forest-skein groups satisfy the topological finiteness
property of being of type F∞ . It is a rare property for (infinite) groups that has pushed the
study of F, T, V : Thompson-like groups are the main source of infinite simple groups with
good finiteness properties such as F∞ , see Section 6.4 for definitions and references. We
end by providing a huge class of explicit forest-skein categories that are all Ore categories
and whose fraction groups are of type F∞ .
As previously mentioned, the framework chosen has been inspired by Jones’ planar al-
gebra, Jones’ reconstruction program of CFT, and Brown’s diagrammatic description of
Thompson’s groups. The presentation does not use much categorical language but is
rather set theoretical and constructive in the vein of the styles of Jones, Brin, and De-
hornoy which inspired the author. On the technical side we use previous work of Dehornoy
on monoids and Thumann on operad groups. The first permits to prove the existence of
certain forest-skein groups and the second to establish a finiteness property.
Detailed plan and main results. In Section 1 we define forest-skein categories and
monoids using presentations (generators and skein relations). Forest-skein categories
are monoidal small categories but we treat them as classical algebraic structures: sets
equipped with two binary operations. We define morphisms between them and in partic-
ular forest subcategories and quotients. Presented forest-skein categories can be alterna-
tively defined as the solution of a universal problem. This permits us to announce how
Jones’ technology will be used. We provide explicit category presentations of forest-skein
categories and monoids.
Section 2 is about left-cancellativity and Ore’s property. These are the two conditions
we want for constructing groups. We start by giving definition and some obvious but
useful reformulations of them. Then we present some more advance techniques due to
Dehornoy that we apply to our specific class of forest-skein categories and monoids. These
techniques provide powerful characterisations and criteria for checking left-cancellativity
of forest-skein categories and in fewer cases Ore’s property. In practice we prove Ore’s
property by explicitly constructing a cofinal sequence of tree.

allows us to prove a permanence property that holds for all forest-skein groups regardless

Section 7 is about Thumann’s theory of operad groups and his finiteness theorem [Thu17].
Thumann formalism and theorem are impressively general. He covers and adapts in a
single categorical approach a number of key techniques pioneered by Brown that have
been refined and extended (in a nontrivial way and using new ideas) to study many more
groups like Stein groups, Brin’s higher dimensional dV , braided Thompson group BV ,
Guba-Sapir diagram groups and Hughes’ finite similarity structure groups, to cite a few
[Bro87, Ste92, FMWZ13, BFM+ 16, FH15]. We refer the reader to the articles of Zaremsky
and Witzel for a first read on these techniques and strategy [Zar21, Wit19]. We prove
that all forest-skein groups are operad groups and establish a dictionary between the two
theories. This allows us to precisely translate and specialise the theorem of Thumann
to forest-skein groups. We define the spine of a forest-skein category F that is roughly
speaking the mcm-closure of the set of trees with two leaves (where mcm stands for
“minimal common multiples”) and deduce the following.
Theorem D. If F is a Ore forest-skein category with a finite spine, then the X-forest-
skein groups Frac(F X , 1) is of type F∞ for X = F, T, V, BV.

technical arguments corresponding to planar, symmetric, and braided operad groups,
respectively. Using our previous theorem we add the missing T -case. Moreover, note
that this theorem covers a class of groups that is closed under taking braiding. This

            1. A class of categories built from coloured forests
In this section we define what is a forest-skein category. This is an algebraic structure
similar than a monoid but with several units (a small category). It is roughly equal to a
collection of forest that can be concatenated and whose vertices have various colours. We
start by fixing the terminology and introducing what we call coloured trees and forests.
From there we introduce free forest-skein categories that are analogues of free monoids but
obeying nontrivial relations induced by the diagrammatic structure. Finally, we introduce
relations and quotients of these free forest-skein categories. This leads to the notion of
skein presentations, forest-skein categories, and morphisms between them.
1.1. Coloured forests and trees.

monochromatic tree T2 . The vertex set of T2 is the set of all finite binary sequences of 0

A monochromatic tree t is a finite rooted subtree of T2 so that each of its vertex has either

(f, f 0 ) providing they have the same number of leaves but also roots. This seems more
general but in fact it is not. Indeed, a relation of the form (f, f 0 ) can be obtained from
the set of all relations (tf, tf 0 ) where t runs over all trees composable with f .
Most of the examples investigate will have S and R finite. In fact, many interesting
examples arise when S has two elements a, b and R has one or two relations.
1.3. Forest-skein category.
1.3.1. Algebraic structure. We now analyse the algebraic structure of the set F equipped
with the two operations ◦, ⊗.
The triple (F, ◦, ⊗) has an obvious structure of a monoidal small category, i.e. a category
whose collections of objects and morphisms are sets and equipped with a monoidal product
(or tensor product). We interpret F as a category for the convenience of the terminology.
Although, we think of F as a classical algebraic structure: a set equipped with two
composition laws.
Categorical structure. The set of object ob(F) of F is the set of natural number
N := {0, 1, 2, · · · } (taking the convention that 0 is a natural number). If m, n ∈ N, then
we write F(m, n) for the set of morphism from m to n equal to all forests having m leaves
20                                  ARNAUD BROTHIER

and n roots. Hence, a morphism is a forest with source its number of leaves and target
its number of roots. Note that this is the opposite convention of [Bro21]. Observe that
F(m, n) is empty if m < n and F(m, m) contains one element: the trivial forest I ⊗m with
m roots. This is the identity automorphism of F(m, m). Moreover, note that F(m, 0)
is empty unless m = 0. (Here we identify the empty forest with the trivial forest with
0 roots.) The composition is associative: it is clear in the free case since it corresponds
in concatenating labelled graphs. The general case follows since it is a free forest-skein
category mod out by an equivalence relation closed under composition. All together we
deduce that (F, ◦) is a small category.
Monoidal structure. The monoidal structure is given by the binary operation ⊗. On
objects it is defined as n ⊗ m := n + m for n, m ∈ N. For morphisms (i.e. forests) it is
defined as above by horizontal concatenations of forests. The tensor unit is the empty
forest. A similar reasoning than above shows that all the axioms of a monoidal category
are satisfied. Hence, (F, ◦, ⊗) is a monoidal small category. We will continue to write
f ∈ F for a forest f and will say that f is an element of F (rather than calling f a
morphism of the category F). Note that the object 0 and the empty diagram are rather
useless. We only added them in order to have a tensor unit and to fulfil precisely the
axioms of a monoidal category.
Definition 1.3.      (1) The monoidal category (F, ◦, ⊗) is called a forest-skein category.
  (2) Let S be a nonempty set and R a set of pairs of coloured trees over S that have
       the same number of leaves. Consider F := ForChS|Ri as defined above and the
       binary operations of composition ◦ and tensor product ⊗. We say that (S, R)
       is a skein presentation, ForChS|Ri a presented forest-skein category, S the set of
       colours, and R the set of skein relations. We may drop the words “forest” and
       ”skein” if the context is clear.
  (3) When the set of relations R is empty we write ForChSi or UFhSi rather than
       ForChS| ∅i and call it the universal forest-skein category or the free forest-skein
       category over the set S where the symbol U stands for “universal”.
It is sometime convenient to discuss about abstract forest-skein categories where a skein
presentation has not been specified. Similarly, we may want to discuss about colours in
the absence of a presentation.
Definition 1.4.         (1) An abstract forest-skein category is a monoidal small category
       (C, ◦, ⊗) with set of object N so that there exist a presented forest-skein category
       ForChS|Ri and a monoidal functor φ : ForChS|Ri → C that is the identity on
       object and is bijective on morphisms. In that case we say that (S, R) is a skein
       presentation of the abstract forest-skein category C.
  (2) An abstract forest-skein category is called free or universal if it admits a presen-
       tation (S 0 , R0 ) where R0 = ∅.
  (3) An abstract colour of an abstract forest-skein category F is a tree with two leaves.
Remark 1.5. We may drop the term abstract if the context is clear. Note that if F =

1.3.2. Forest-skein monoids. Infinite forests. An infinite forest f (over F) is a sequence
of trees (fn : n ≥ 1). Pictorially, we imagine f has an infinite planar diagram contained in

forests.

support of f is the set of n ≥ 1 satisfying that fn is not the trivial tree (the tree with

1.5. The collection of all forest-skein categories. Our main object of study is a
forest-skein category considered as an algebraic structure like a monoid or a group. We
will now define what are the right notion of maps between them. This leads us to introduce
the category of forest-skein categories. This is a category that is not captured by classical
set theory and we treat it in that way. The notion of morphism between forest-skein
categories may seem restrictive at a first glance. However, it is the correct notion assuring
that range of morphisms are forest-skein categories (leading to the notions of quotient and
embedding). We could spend more time defining various operations and constructions in
this category (like free product, direct product, etc.) but leave it to future articles when
we will derive applications of these operations.
1.5.1. The category of forest-skein categories. Collection of objects. Let Forest be the
collection of all forest-skein categories. This is not a set since the class of all nonempty
sets embeds in it via S 7→ UFhSi.
Morphisms. Consider two forest-skein categories F, F̃. A morphism φ from F to F̃ is
a covariant monoidal functor from F to F̃ satisfying that φ(1) = 1 for the object 1. In
other terms, φ is a map from F to F̃ such that if f is a forest with n roots and m leaves,
then φ(f ) is a forest of F̃ with n roots and m leaves. Moreover, φ(f ◦ g) = φ(f ) ◦ φ(g)
and φ(f ⊗ h) = φ(f ) ⊗ φ(h) for all f, g, h ∈ F. We write Hom(F, F̃) for the class of
                                  FOREST-SKEIN GROUPS I                                   25

morphisms form F to F̃. Elements of Hom(F, F̃) are typically written using the symbols
φ, ϕ, ψ, χ. Note, since there is only one tree with one leaf in each forest-skein category
we have φ(I) = I and thus φ sends trivial forests to trivial forests. Similarly, it maps
abstract colours to abstract colours.
Proposition 1.13. The collection of all forest-skein categories together with the collection
of morphisms defines a category that we call the category of forest-skein categories denoted
Forest.
Proof. A composition of covariant monoidal functor is a covariant monoidal functor im-
plying that a composition of morphisms is a morphism. Since morphisms are functions
it is obvious that the composition is associative. For each forest-skein category we can
consider the identity map which defines an identity functor. All together we deduce that
Forest is a category.                                                                  

morphisms. Hence, we will say that φ : F → F̃ is injective or surjective rather than φ is a
monomorphism or an epimorphism, respectively. It is not hard to see that if a morphism
φ : F → F̃ is bijective, then the inverse map is a morphism. Hence, an isomorphism
from F to F̃ is a bijective morphism φ : F → F̃ with inverse written φ−1 . Similarly, an
automorphism of F is a bijective endomorphism of F.

                   2. Ore forest-skein categories and monoids
Our main objective is to construct groups from forest-skein categories using a left-cancellative
calculus of fractions. This is equivalent for a category to be left-cancellative and to sat-
isfy a property due to Ore, see [GZ67, Chapter I] or [DDG+ 15, Chapter 3] for details. A
category with these two properties will be called a Ore category.
In this section we investigate left-cancellativity and Ore’s property. First, we define and
rephrase them via obvious but useful characterisations. Second, we provide more advanced
techniques to decide if a forest-skein category is left-cancellative and in some fewer cases
satisfies Ore’s property. This second analysis is an adaptation of the work of Dehornoy
on complete presentations of monoids [Deh03]. It will provide very useful and powerful
criteria that can be, in some cases, directly read from the skein presentation.
2.1. Definitions and obvious characterisations.
2.1.1. Cancellative. A category C is left-cancellative if f ◦ g = f ◦ h implies g = h. Right-
cancellativity is defined analogously and cancellative means left and right cancellative.
By choice we will only consider the property of being left-cancellative. Here are some
straightforward but useful observations.
Observation 2.1. Consider a forest-skein category F and its associated forest-skein
monoid F∞ . For each f ∈ F we consider the (partially defined) multiplication map:
Lf : g 7→ f ◦ g. The following assertions are equivalent.
   (1) The forest-skein category F is left-cancellative;
   (2) The forest-skein monoid F∞ is left-cancellative;
   (3) The map Lt is injective for all tree t of F;
   (4) The map Ls is injective for all tree s of F with two leaves;
   (5) Any forest subcategory of F (including F itself ) is left-cancellative.
Example 2.2.        (1) A free forest-skein category is cancellative.
                                   FOREST-SKEIN GROUPS I                                        29

   (2) The forest-skein category ForCha, b|a1 b1 = b1 a1 , a1 a2 = b1 b2 i is not left-cancellative.
       Indeed, Ya (Ya ⊗ Yb ) = Ya (Yb ⊗ Ya ) while Ya ⊗ Yb 6= Yb ⊗ Ya .
   (3) A monochromatic forest-skein category is left-cancellative if and only if it is free.
       Here is a proof. Let χ : UF → F be the quotient morphism of the monochromatic
       free forest-skein category onto a monochromatic forest-skein category. Assume
       χ has a nontrivial kernel. Consider a pair of trees (t, s) such that t 6= s and
       χ(t) = χ(s). Moreover, choose this pair (t, s) so that the number of leaves of t is
       minimal. Such a pair exists by assumption. Since there is only one monochromatic
       tree Y with two leaves we necessarily have Y ≤ t, Y ≤ s and they both decomposes
       as follows:
                              t = Y ◦ (h ⊗ k) and s = Y ◦ (h0 ⊗ k 0 )
       with h, k, h0 , k 0 some monochromatic trees not all trivial. Since χ(t) = χ(s) and χ is
       monoidal we deduce that χ(h) = χ(h0 ) and χ(k) = χ(k 0 ). Therefore, (h, h0 ), (k, k 0 )
       are elements of the kernel of χ and are trees with stritcly less leaves than t. This
       implies that h = h0 and k = k 0 implying t = s, a contradiction.
   (4) The forest-skein category ForCha, b|an1 = b1 b2 · · · bn i is left-cancellative for any

       colours and one relations of the form a1 · · · = b1 · · · are left-cancellative.
2.1.2. Ore’s property. A category C satisfies Ore’s property if for all f, g ∈ C with same
target there exists p, q ∈ C satisfying f ◦ p = g ◦ q.
Remark 2.3. Recall that a forest-skein category F is equipped with a partial order ≤
defined as: f ≤ f 0 if there exists p ∈ F satisfying f 0 = f ◦ p. Observe that F satisfies
Ore’s property if and only if given f, g ∈ F with the same number of roots there exists
h ∈ F satisfying that f ≤ h and g ≤ h. We may witness this property by only looking
at the set T of trees. We deduce that the poset of (T , ≤) of trees is directed if and only
if F satisfies Ore’s property.
Definition 2.4. We say that a sequence of trees (tn )n≥1 is cofinal if it is increasing
(i.e. n ≤ m implies tn ≤ tm ) and if for any t ∈ T there exists k ≥ 1 so that t ≤ tk . More
generally, we consider cofinal nets of trees.
Here are useful observations and characterisations of Ore’s property.
Observation 2.5. Let F be a forest-skein category, F∞ its associated forest-skein monoid,
and T ⊂ F the subset of trees equipped with the usual partial order ≤. The following
assertions are equivalent.
   (1) The forest-skein category F satisfies Ore’s property;
   (2) The forest-skein monoid F∞ satisfies Ore’s property;
   (3) The poset (T , ≤) is directed;
   (4) The poset (T , ≤) admits a cofinal net;
   (5) Every quotient of F (including F itself ) satisfies Ore’s property.
If F(2, 1) is countable (which implies that F is countable), then the fourth item can be
replaced by “(T , ≤) admits a cofinal sequence”.
Example 2.6.     (1) Consider a nonempty set S and the free forest-skein category UF
     over S. We have that UF does not satisfies Ore’s property unless S has only one
     point. Indeed, if a, b ∈ S, a 6= b, then there are no tree dominating both Ya and Yb
     inside UF.
30                                      ARNAUD BROTHIER

     (2) The forest-skein category F = ForCha, b|a1 b1 = b1 a1 i satisfies Ore’s property and
         thus so does its quotient ForCha, b|a1 b1 = b1 a1 , a1 a2 = b1 b2 i. Note that the first

     (3) The forest-skein category ForCha, b|an1 = b1 b2 · · · bn i satisfies Ore’s property for

Definition 2.7. A small category C that is left-cancellative and satisfies Ore’s property
is called a Ore category. In that case we say that C admits a (left-cancellative) calculus
of fractions.
We will see in Section 3 how to construct (fraction) groupoids and groups from Ore
forest-skein categories.
Warning. In the literature, several different meanings are given to the term Ore category.
It usually translates the existence of a calculus of fractions but requiring weaker or stronger
conditions than ours.

2.2. Dehornoy’s criteria. We present criteria due to Dehornoy which given a presen-
tation provides tools to decide if the associated algebraic structure is left-cancellative or
satisfies Ore’s property. We adapt and specialise these criteria to our situation of skein
presentations. Most details about these notions can be found in [Deh03]. Although, we
will need several statements from other articles of Dehornoy that we will precisely cite.
Notations. In all this section σ is a nonempty set of letters or generators, word(σ) denotes
the finite words in σ including the trivial one denoted by e. The length |u| = length(u)
of a word u is the number of letters that composed u taking the convention |e| = 0. We
write ρ for a set of pairs (u, v) ∈ word(σ)2 satisfying |u| = |v|. Elements of ρ are called
relations. We consider the pair π := (σ, ρ) that we interpret as the presentation of a
monoid M that we denote by Monhσ|ρi where Mon stands for monoid. We continue to
write u ∈ word(σ) for its image in the monoid M and may write u =ρ v to express that
two words u, v ∈ word(σ) are equal in the monoid M , i.e. (u, v) belongs to the smallest
equivalence relation generated by ρ.
Terminology. We say that π is a homogeneous monoid presentation. This means that
all relations of ρ are pairs of words (u, u0 ) in the letter set σ such that |u| = |u0 |. In
particular, u does not contain any inverse of letters of σ (it is a monoid presentation).
This last property is also called positive.
As usual we write σ −1 for the set of formal inverses of elements of σ. We consider the
group G = Grhσ|ρi with presentation (σ, ρ) where Gr stands for group.

                                  3. Forest-skein groups
Fix a Ore forest-skein category F. We will present the calculus of fractions of F. This
will allow us to define a groupoid Frac(F) and in particular its isotropy groups Frac(F, n)
that are all (equivalence classes of) pairs of forests with the same number of leaves and
a fixed number of roots n. We will be particularly interested in Frac(F, 1) defined from
pairs of trees.

3.1. Fraction groupoids of forest-skein categories. We recall the construction of the
fraction groupoid of F. Extensive details can be found in [GZ67]. All statements below
are standard and easy exercises.
Pairs of forests. Let PF be the set of pairs (f, g) ∈ F × F where f and g have the same
number of leaves. Consider the smallest equivalence relation ∼ of PF generated by
                       (f, g) ∼ (f ◦ h, g ◦ h) for all (f, g) ∈ PF , h ∈ F
and write Frac(F) for the quotient PF / ∼. We write [f, g] for the equivalence class of
(f, g) inside Frac(F).
A binary operation: fraction groupoids. We define a partially define binary oper-
ation on Frac(F). Consider (f, g), (f 0 , g 0 ) ∈ PF such that | Root(g)| = | Root(f 0 )|. By
Ore’s property there exists p, p0 ∈ F satisfying gp = f 0 p0 . We define a binary operation
as follows:
                                 [f, g] ◦ [f 0 , g 0 ] := [f p, g 0 p0 ].
We recall well-known facts on calculus of fractions that we specialize to our context.
Proposition 3.1. The following assertions are true.
  (1) The binary operation ◦ of above is indeed well-defined and associative (it does not
      depend on the choices of p, p0 nor on the representatives (f, g), (f 0 , g 0 ));
  (2) The algebraic structure (Frac(F), ◦) is a groupoid;
  (3) The formula f 7→ [f, I ⊗n ], where n = | Leaf(f )|, defines a covariant functor from
      F to Frac(F) (it preserves compositions).
  (4) We have the identities [f, g]◦[g, h] = [f, h] and [f, g]−1 = [g, f ] for all [f, g], [g, h] ∈
      Frac(F).
Definition 3.2. We call Frac(F) the fraction groupoid of F and its elements fractions.
A forest-skein groupoid is a groupoid G for which there exists a forest-skein category F
satisfying that G ≃ Frac(F).
Remark 3.3. Note that the fraction groupoid Frac(F) is obtained by formally inverting
elements of F and is sometimes denoted by F[F −1 ]. Formally, [f, g] corresponds to f ◦ g −1
and we shall often use this notation which makes item 3 and 4 obvious. It is common to
denote [f, g] as a fraction fg by analogy with fractions of real numbers or more generally
elements of localized rings. This explains the terminology. Every small category admits
an enveloping groupoid made of signed-path of morphisms. What is exceptional here is
that being a Ore category assures that every element (or morphism) of this enveloping
groupoid (which is the fraction groupoid) is a word of at most length two: f ◦ g −1 .
Note that the map F → Frac(F), f 7→ [f, I ⊗n ] where n = | Leaf(f )| is not injective in
general. Indeed, for a Ore forest-skein category it is injective if and only if F is also
right-cancellative (that is F is left and right-cancellatives not only left-cancellative), see
Lemma 3.8 and Proposition 3.11 in [DDG+ 15]. Even weaker conditions can be considered
for having a right-calculus of fractions (for instance by requiring a weaker form of left-
cancellativity) but we will not consider them in our study, see [GZ67].

3.2. Fraction groups associated to forest-skein categories. We are interested in
constructing and studying groups rather than groupoids. Given F as above and its
fraction groupoid Frac(F) we can consider for each r ≥ 1 the set of pairs (f, g) where
f, g have r roots and have the same number of leaves. The set of such classes [f, g] is
a group that we write Frac(F, r). If we consider Frac(F) using categorical terminology,
then Frac(F, r) is nothing else than the automorphism group of the object r inside the
groupoid Frac(F) also called the isotropy group of the object r.
Definition 3.4. Let F be a Ore forest-skein category. The fraction group of F is
Frac(F, 1). It is the set of fractions [f, g] = f ◦ g −1 where f, g are trees with the same
number of leaves equipped with the restriction of the multiplication of Frac(F). A forest-
skein group is a group G isomorphic to the fraction group of a forest-skein category. We
may also say that Frac(F, 1) is the forest-skein group associated to F.
Example 3.5. Let F be the monochromatic free forest-skein category. It is left-cancellative
and satisfies Ore’s property. Its fraction group Frac(F, 1) is (isomorphic to) Thompson’s
group F via the Brown diagrammatic description of [Bro87]. Moreover, Frac(F, r) for
r ≥ 1 is the Higman-Thompson’s group usually denoted by F2,r in the literature. Note
40                                   ARNAUD BROTHIER

that F2,r is the “F -version” of the Higman-Thompson’s group V2,r that was first defined
by Brown in [Bro87]. More examples can be found in Section 3.6 and Section 8.
Remark 3.6. It is worth noting that if F is a Ore forest-skein category, then Frac(F, r) is
isomorphic to Frac(F, 1) for all r ≥ 1. Indeed, since we are considering binary forests there
exists a tree t with r leaves. Now, the conjugation by t provides a group isomorphism
from Frac(F, r) to Frac(F, 1). In categorical language, the groupoid Frac(F) is path-
connected implying that all its isotropy groups are isomorphic. (If we add the empty
diagram and the object 0 to F in the purpose of having a tensor unit and fulfilling
scrupulously all the axioms of a monoidal category, we obtain one additional isolated
path-connected component associated to 0 giving the trivial isotropy group Frac(F, 0).) If
we were considering ternary forests rather than binary, then there would be no morphisms
between 1 and 2 for instance. Although, for the classical case of ternary forests with one
colour and no quotients the Higman-Thompson’s groups F3,r , r ≥ 1 are all isomorphic but
for a different reason, see [Bro87, Proposition 4.1].
3.3. Functoriality. The collection of all Ore forest-skein categories together with mor-
phisms between them forms a subcategory of Forest that we write ForestOre and call the
category of Ore forest-skein categories. We collect straightforward facts concerning mor-
phisms between forest-skein categories, fraction groupoids, and fraction groups. It shows
that all our constructions are functorial. They are all easy to prove and well-known in
greater generality.
Proposition 3.7. Let F, G be Ore forest-skein categories. The following assertions are
true.
    (1) If θ ∈ Hom(F, G), then θ uniquely extends into groupoid and group morphisms
           Frac(θ) : Frac(F) → Frac(G) and Frac(θ, 1) : Frac(F, 1) → Frac(G, 1)
         via the formula
                                     [f, g] 7→ [θ(f ), θ(g)].
         We often write θ for Frac(θ) or Frac(θ, 1) if the context is clear.
     (2) The assignments F 7→ Frac(F), θ 7→ Frac(θ) and F 7→ Frac(F, 1), θ 7→ Frac(θ, 1)
         define covariant functors that we write:
               Frac : ForestOre → Groupoid and Frac(−, 1) : ForestOre → Gr
         where Groupoid is the category of groupoids and Gr the category of groups.
     (3) If a morphism θ : F → G is injective (resp. surjective), then Frac(θ) and Frac(θ, 1)
         are both injective (resp. surjective).

3.6.4. Brin’s higher dimensional Thompson groups. Brin’s groups dV with d ≥ 1 are
higher dimensional generalisation of Thompson’s group V so that 1V = V and dV acts
by piecewise affine maps on the hypercube [0, 1]d . An element of dV is characterised
by two standard dyadic partitions of [0, 1]d : P = {R1 , · · · , Rk } and P 0 = {R10 , · · · , Rk0 }
having the same number of pieces and one bijection β between the elements of P and P 0 .
The pieces Rj are higher dimensional rectangle obtained by iteratively cutting the unit
hypercube into two equal pieces along one axis. We now describe a description of elements
of dV using trees given by Brin and further exploit by Burillo and Cleary [Bri04, BC10].
We can encode the data of P, P 0 as a pair of trees with d possible colours of carets, each
colour corresponding to one axis. Now, if a, b are two colours, then the two operations
Ya (Yb ⊗ Yb ) and Yb (Ya ⊗ Ya ) provide the same result of cutting the hypercube into four
using two different axis. We define the forest-skein category F with d colours and relations
a1 (b1 b3 ) = b1 (a1 a3 ) for all pairs of colours a, b. We obtain a Ore forest-skein category.
Although, its fraction group is not Brin’s group dV but share similar metric properties.
What makes it different is that the skein relations are not compatible with bijections β,
see [BC10, Section 1] for details.
Note that the decolouring map

simple (as proved by Brin in [Bri04] for d = 2 and in the general case in [Bri10]) we deduce
that they are not isomorphic to our forest-skein groups.

                                    5. Betti numbers
In this short section we show that every countable forest-skein groups have trivial first
`2 -Betti number. This investigation comes after a discussion with Sri (Srivatsav Kun-
nawalkam Elayavalli) in Bonn at the Hausdorff Institute of Mathematics in September
2022. Sri strongly suspected that forest-skein groups have trivial first `2 -Betti number
and moreover suggested a proof based on the notion of ”good list of generators” as we
name it below. We warmly thank Sri!
Convention. As in the rest of this article all groups appearing in this section are discrete
but moreover are assumed to be countable.
The notion of `2 -Betti numbers for groups is due to Atiyah, later generalised by Cheeger
and Gromov, and extensively studied by Lück and Gaboriau [Ati76, CG86, Luc02, Gab02].
Using Lück’s work and in particular his generalisation of Murray-von Neumann’s dimen-
sion we can define the `2 -Betti numbers of a countable discrete group G as:
                          βn(2) (G) := dimLG Hn (G; LG) for n ≥ 0
where LG stands for the group von Neumann algebra of G, dimLG for the Lück dimension
                                                                                  (2)
and Hn (G; LG) for the n-th homology group over the G-module LG. Note that βn (G)
is a positive real number (so possibly not a natural number). Indeed, non-integers may
appear as well as irrational and even transcendental, see [PSZ15] and the discussion on
the Atiyah problem, see also [LW13, Gra14].
Our strategy is based on the more recent work of Peterson and Thom where they rephrase
this numbers to dimension of cohomology groups:
                                βn(2) (G) = dimLG H n (G, M )
where M is the G-module either equal to the densely defined closed operators UG affiliated
to LG, the group von Neumann algebra LG, or the Hilbert space `2 (G), see [PT11, Section
                                      (2)
2]. In particular, the first number β1 (G) is the Lück dimension of the group of 1-cocycles
valued in M = UG, LG, or `2 (G) mod out by the inner ones. It is known that this
dimension is zero if and only if H 1 (G, UG) is trivial , see [Tho08] and [PT11, Section 2].
Note that Lück proved that all the `2 -Betti numbers of Thompson’s group F are trivial
and via a different proof Bader, Furman, and Sauer recovered the result of Lück that they
extended to T , see [Luc02, Theorem 7.10] and [BFS14, Theorem 1.8].

6.4. A finiteness result. We recall few definitions of topological finiteness properties
of group introduced by Wall [Wal65]. There exist homological analogous that we don’t
present here as we did not find any applications with them. We refer the reader to the
book of Geoghegan [Geo07, Sections 7 and 8] for details.
Classifying space and universal cover. Let G be a group. A classifying space BG
of G (or K(G, 1)-complex) is a (pointed) path-connected CW complex such that all its
homotopy groups are trivial except the first one π1 (BG, p) (the Poincaré group) which is
isomorphic to G. A universal cover EG of BG is necessarily contractible admitting a free
(cellular) action G y EG whose quotient G\EG is isomorphic to BG. Conversely, every
classifying space arise in that way. All classifying spaces of a fixed group G are pairwise
homotopically equivalent but they may not be isomorphic as complexes. Hence, we may
define invariants of the group using properties of the family of its classifying spaces.
Topological finiteness properties. If G is a group and n ≥ 0, then we say that G
satisfies the topological finiteness property Fn (or simply is of type Fn ) if there exists a
classifying space BG with finite n-skeleton (i.e. BG has finitely many k-cells for all k ≤ n).
Equivalently, G is of type Fn if there exists a contractible CW complex EG on which G
acts freely and such that there are only finitely many k-cells modulo G for each k ≤ n.
The group G is of type F∞ if it is of type Fn for all n ≥ 1. Note that all groups are of type
F0 admitting a classifying space with one vertex. Moreover, being of type F1 (resp. F2 )
is equivalent to be finitely generated (resp. finitely presented).
Geometric dimension. The geometric dimension dimg (G) of G is the minimal dimen-
sion (as a complex) of a classifying space of G which is either a natural number or infinity
∞. It is increasing with respect to inclusion of groups and satisfies that dimg (Zr ) = r
and dimg (Z/kZ) = ∞ for any k ≥ 2.
Exceptional properties of Thompson-like groups. The group F was the first ex-
ample of a torsion-free group with infinite geometric dimension and of type F∞ as proved
by Brown and Geoghegan [BG84]. Since the result of Brown-Geoghegan there have been
many groups similar to F that have been proved to be of type F∞ . Here is a list that
64                                  ARNAUD BROTHIER

is very far from being exhaustive which includes the family of groups: T, V , Higman-
Thompson’s groups, Stein’s groups, Brin’s higher dimensional Thompson’s groups dV ,
Brin and Dehornoy’s braided Thompson’s group BV , and large subclasses of Hughes’
FSS groups, Guba-Sapir’s diagram groups, Rover-Nekrashevych’s groups, cloning system
of groups, operad groups, and a number of other generalisations [Bro87, Ste92, KMPN13,
FMWZ13, BFM+ 16, FH15, WZ18, Thu17]. We refer to a recent preprint of Skipper and
Wu for additional examples and references [SW21].
For all these families of groups the scheme of the proof is based on two technical results:
Brown’s criterion and Bestvina-Brady’s discrete Morse theory [Bro87, BB97]. It relies
on finding a suitable filtration of a CW complex (often a simplicial one) and computing
connectivity of links rather than finding a CW complex with finite skeleton (which is rare
to find in practice). We refer the reader to the article of Zaremsky for a very clear and
pedagogical explanation on this strategy [Zar21].
Even though many families of Thompson-like groups have been proved to be either not
finitely presented or of type F∞ there exist groups satisfying properties in between. Indeed,
for any n ≥ 1 there exists a Thompson-like groups that is of type Fn but not of type Fn+1
using a construction and result of Tanushevski [Tan16]. This is obtained by considering
diagrams of monochromatic binary forests and by decorating their leaves with elements of
a group with the desirable finiteness property. Although, all these examples are nontrivial
split extension of Thompson group (F, T , or V ) and are thus not simple. Skipper, Witzel,
and Zaremsky have constructed a sequence of Rover-Nekrashevych groups that are of type
Fn but not of type Fn+1 and moreover are simple (giving the first family of this kind), see
[SWZ19] and also the family of groups constructed by Belk and Zaremsky [BZ22].
Note that all forest-skein groups have infinite geometric dimension since they contain a
copy of F , see Corollary 3.9. Although, it is a delicate question to decide if they have
finiteness properties. Indeed, finiteness properties (topological or homological) fail to be
close under most permanence properties.
A result. We adapt a clever strategy due to Brown and Geoghegan explained in [Bro87,
Section 4B, Remark 2] and in the book [Geo07, Theorem 9.4.2] to deduce that GT is of
type Fn when G is. To do this we use a criteria stated by Brown [Bro87, Proposition 1.1]
on homological finiteness property which we simplify and translate to our need.
Proposition 6.3. Consider n ≥ 1, a group G, and C a contractible CW-complex. If
there exists a cellular action G y C such that the stabiliser of each cell is of type Fn and
C has finitely many k-cells modulo G for each k ≥ 0, then G is a group of type Fn .

7.2.2. Strategy and limits of Thumann’s theorem. Strategy of Thumann. The strategy
of Thumann resides in considering the exact same complex than our: EF the geometric
realisation of the ordered complex of the directed poset (Q, ≤) where Q is the set of [t, f ]
with t a tree and f a forest. He considers the origin map ω : Q → N, [t, f ] 7→ | Root(f )|
that is extended as a Morse function ω : EF → R+ by making it affine on each simplex.
This map provides a filtration EFj := ω −1 ([0, j]) that is compatible with the (left) action
of G := Frac(F, 1). Now, G\EFj is a finite complex and thus by Brown’s criterion
we have that G is of type F∞ if the connectivity of the pair (EFj+1 , EFj ) tends to
infinity in j. Using Bestvina-Brady discrete Morse theory it is then sufficient to prove
that the descending links link↓ (v) in EF of vertices v ∈ EF (0) = Q has its connectivity
tending to infinity when ω(v) tends to infinity. This last part is the technical core of the
proof. One can show that link↓ (v) only depends on ω(v) = j and is isomorphic to some
complex Lj . Now, one can decompose Lj with respect to subcomplexes related to Lk for
k < j. The Hurewicz theorem and the Mayer-Vietoris sequence give some lower bound
for the connectivity of Lj with respect to the connectivity of some Lk with k < j. When
good hypothesis on F are added (in this case: its spine is finite) one can deduce that
the connectivity of Lj tends to infinity and thus G is of type F∞ . Note, this proof is
performed separately for the three cases of planar, symmetric, and braided operad groups
corresponding to F and its V and BV -versions, respectively.
Garside family and Witzel’s theorem. There is another important strategy that can
be used for this kind of groups. The idea is to find a smaller subcomplex of X ⊂ EF
that is stabilised by G and still contractible (so a universal cover of a classifying space
of G). We then apply a similar strategy to X using Brown’s criterion and Bestvina-
Brady discrete Morse theory. If X is significantly smaller than EF, then proving that
the connectivity of descending links are getting large may be easier to do.
Dehornoy defined Garside family for monoids and more generally for certain small cate-
gories [DDG+ 15]. Witzel proved (in greater generality) the key result that if we keep the
same vertex set Q but allow only simplices of the form x < xf1 < · · · < xfn where all fj
are in a fixed factor-closed Garside family (or equivalently fn is in this specified Garside
family), then we obtain a subcomplex X ⊂ EF that is G-closed and still contractible
[Wit19]. One can then apply the strategy of above to X to deduce finiteness properties
of F. Of course, the smaller the Garside family is, the smaller X is, and the better is the
theorem of Witzel for doing computations.
                                    FOREST-SKEIN GROUPS I                                   71

Using Dehornoy’s work we have that every Ore forest-skein category F admits a smaller
Garside family Gar(F): it is the smallest set containing the abstract colours of F that is
closed under taking mcm and right-factors. To fulfil Witzel’s assumption we close it for
left-factors as well and continue to write it Gar(F). If Γ(F) denotes the trees of Gar(F),
then note that Gar(F) is the set of forest f = (f1 , · · · , fn ) such that fj ∈ Γ(F) for each
1 ≤ j ≤ n. By definition, we observe that Spine(F) ⊂ Γ(F). Hence, if the spine is infinite,
then we cannot apply Thumann’s result but we can still use Witzel strategy by taking
the subcomplex X. The complex X will be still quite large since Γ(F) is infinite but we
may still hope to simplify a number of computations.
One rebel example. We present one example of forest-skein category that has a small
skein presentation with two colours and two relations of length two, provides a forest-skein
group very similar to F , but does not fulfil the assumption of Thumann’s theorem and
for which Witzel’s theorem does not help. Consider the forest-skein category

However, we will see in a future article that its forest-skein group G := Frac(F, 1) is of

of the form Z2 oX F where F y X is the usual action of F on the dyadic rationals of [0, 1).
The group G can be described as the usual Thompson group F using pairs of trees (with a
single type of caret) but where leaves are labelled by 0 or 1 (the elements of Z/2Z). From
this point of view it is somehow the most elementary forest-skein group strictly larger than
F that we could think of. However, it has infinite spine and its Garside family Gar(F) si

                     8. A large class of forest-skein groups
As stressed before, given a monoid or more generally a category it is a difficult task to
check if it is left-cancellative or satisfies Ore’s property. In this section, we will provide
a large class of examples of forest-skein categories that are all Ore categories and thus
produce forest-skein groups. These categories have skein presentations of a particular
kind built from a family of monochromatic trees. The monochromaticity makes possible
to prove at once that they all satisfy the property of Ore. Moreover, using a general
criteria of Dehornoy we prove they are left-cancellative. Finally, their spine is as small
as it could be: equal to the set of trees with two leaves and a single additional tree. We
start by giving the general construction and then specialise to the two colours case.
